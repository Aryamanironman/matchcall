# Literature Review — Narrative Synthesis

**Project:** football-commentator (Aryaman)  
**Date:** 27 October 2026  
**Companion document:** `docs/literature_review.md` (verified reading list, comparison table, search strategy)

## 1. Introduction

Automatically turning a broadcast football match into spoken commentary requires several kinds of understanding at once: locating events in time, identifying who is involved, deciding what is worth saying, saying it in fluent and grounded language, and delivering it with appropriate timing and prosody. Research on these subproblems has advanced largely in separate communities — action spotting and tracking in computer vision, commentary generation in NLP and multimedia, and speech synthesis in speech processing. This review synthesises those literatures and identifies the gap that the present project addresses: a deployable pipeline that keeps perception, fact selection, language generation, and speech synthesis separable, auditable, and reproducible on consumer hardware.

The review is organised around the pipeline. Section 2 covers action spotting and event understanding; Section 3 covers detection, tracking, team identification, and possession; Section 4 traces commentary generation from templates to neural and LLM-based systems; Section 5 discusses grounding and evaluation; Section 6 covers speech synthesis and prosody; Section 7 synthesises the gaps and positions the project.

## 2. Action spotting and event understanding

SoccerNet established the modern formulation of action spotting: given a long broadcast video, predict the temporal location and class of sparse events such as goals, cards, and substitutions (Giancola et al., 2018). Its Average-mAP metric evaluates predictions at multiple temporal tolerances, and the loose/tight distinction matters for commentary because a goal called ten seconds late is a different failure from one called one second late. SoccerNet-v2 expanded the taxonomy to seventeen action classes and added camera-shot segmentation and replay grounding (Deliège et al., 2021), making the benchmark closer to the structure of a real broadcast.

Methods have moved from pre-extracted features to temporally aware and end-to-end models. CALF introduced a context-aware loss that models the temporal neighbourhood of sparse anchors and improved the SoccerNet baseline substantially (Cioppa et al., 2020). NetVLAD++ pooled past and future context separately and became a strong, efficient feature-aggregation baseline (Giancola & Ghanem, 2021). E2E-Spot showed that a compact end-to-end model trained on short clips can produce precise timestamps on a single GPU (Hong et al., 2022), which is relevant to settings where compute is limited. The SoccerNet challenge reports document steady progress, with tight Average-mAP reaching the low 70s in 2023 and dense captioning improving with LLM-based approaches (Cioppa et al., 2023; 2024). ASTRA is notable for using audio to recognise non-visible actions and for placing third in the 2023 challenge (Xarles et al., 2023).

Two lessons follow. First, temporal precision is a first-class requirement, not a cosmetic one. Second, the strongest systems are trained on large, well-annotated broadcast corpora; a lightweight system that relies on broadcast graphics and a small event classifier is a different point in the design space. The present project adopts that lightweight point deliberately: a score-bug reader supplies high-precision goal anchors, a motion-gated window scan proposes candidate events, and a ResNet-18 classifier assigns labels. The literature suggests this will be less accurate than a dedicated spotting network on rare classes, but it is inspectable and runs on the target GPU.

## 3. Detection, tracking, team identification, and possession

The perception layer of a commentary system needs more than event timestamps: it needs players, the ball, teams, and possession. YOLO established single-pass, real-time object detection (Redmon et al., 2016), and the Ultralytics family provides a practical deployment path with tracking modes built on ByteTrack or BoT-SORT. ByteTrack's key idea — associating low-score detection boxes rather than discarding them — is particularly relevant to broadcast football, where occlusion and motion blur produce many low-confidence boxes (Zhang et al., 2022).

Sports-specific benchmarks expose how difficult this setting is. SportsMOT contains 240 sequences across football, basketball, and volleyball, with fast motion and similar appearances (Cui et al., 2023). SoccerNet-Tracking provides 200 thirty-second sequences plus a full 45-minute half, with boxes and tracklet identities (Cioppa et al., 2022). These benchmarks make clear that generic multi-object tracking metrics such as MOTA, IDF1, and HOTA are necessary but not sufficient: a commentary system also needs team identity and possession, which are semantic quantities rather than track identities.

Work on jersey-colour team identification and possession is therefore directly relevant. DeepPlayer-Track combines YOLOv4 with jersey-colour masks and a tracking-by-detection pipeline (Naik et al., 2022). More recently, Guo et al. (2026) combine YOLOv8 variants, DBSCAN jersey clustering, and Norfair tracking to estimate possession over full-match clips, reporting an RMSE of 4.87. These systems support the project's design choice to cluster jersey colours without labelled team identities and to derive possession from the nearest tracked player to the ball. The limitation is that possession remains a heuristic: broadcast camera cuts, replays, and close-ups break the spatial continuity on which it depends. The project's bounded ball interpolation and rejection of single-frame teleports are a response to exactly this failure mode.

## 4. From templates to neural and LLM commentary

The earliest automatic soccer commentary systems were rule-based. MIKE used six analysers, importance-scored proposition selection, template-based natural-language generation, and speech synthesis (Tanaka-Ishii et al., 1998). Its architecture remains instructive: it separated the decision of *what to say* from the decision of *how to say it*. Much later work has more powerful surface realisation but often less explicit fact selection.

SoccerNet-Caption introduced dense video captioning for soccer broadcasts, with almost 37,000 timestamped commentaries across 715.9 hours and single-anchor captioning evaluated with METEOR in a ±15 s window (Mkhallati et al., 2023). MatchTime addressed a central weakness of raw broadcast commentary: the commentary is not perfectly aligned with the video. It introduced a manually timestamped alignment set, a temporal alignment pipeline, the MatchTime dataset, and the MatchVoice model (Rao et al., 2024). The lesson is that alignment quality is a first-order determinant of generation quality; a model trained on misaligned pairs learns to describe the wrong moment.

More recent systems use larger multimodal models and retrieval. TimeSoccer takes a full 45-minute match as input, compresses frames with MoFA-Select, and performs single-anchor dense captioning (You et al., 2025). SoccerComment combines LanguageBind encoders, a Vicuna-7B model, and retrieval-augmented in-context learning (Li et al., 2025). These systems produce richer language than template systems, but they are heavy: full-match multimodal inference and 7B-parameter models are difficult to reproduce on consumer hardware. Ravuru (2025) makes the compute constraint explicit in a study of commentary generation for soccer highlights, documenting hardware limitations and window-size effects. The present project takes the opposite trade-off: a GPT-2 model with LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022; Radford et al., 2019), trained on a grounded corpus, with a deterministic template fallback. This is less fluent than a 7B MLLM, but it is trainable and runnable on a small GPU, and its failure modes are visible.

## 5. Grounding, hallucination, and evaluation

The central risk in neural commentary is hallucination: a fluent line that invents a player, team, score, or event. The NLP literature provides both a taxonomy and a measurement approach. Ji et al. (2023) survey hallucination in natural-language generation, distinguishing intrinsic from extrinsic errors. FActScore evaluates long-form generation by decomposing it into atomic facts and checking each against a knowledge source (Min et al., 2023). Retrieval-augmented generation supplies external evidence at generation time (Lewis et al., 2020), but retrieval alone does not guarantee that every generated claim is supported.

Football commentary has no dedicated faithfulness benchmark of the kind FActScore provides for biographies. SoccerNet-Caption, MatchTime, TimeSoccer, and SoccerComment report captioning-overlap or alignment metrics; BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, and CIDEr measure similarity to references, not factual correctness (Papineni et al., 2002; Lin, 2004; Banerjee & Lavie, 2005; Vedantam et al., 2015). A line can score well on overlap while inventing a scorer. This is the gap the project's validator targets: a deterministic fact layer records the score, minute, ball zone, possession, tracked-player count, and event team, and generated lines are checked against it before being accepted; rejected lines fall back to grounded templates. The approach is closer to FActScore's atomic checking than to retrieval-only grounding, and it makes the acceptance rate reportable.

Evaluation of the full pipeline is necessarily multi-metric. Action spotting uses Average-mAP at tight and loose tolerances; tracking uses HOTA, MOTA, and IDF1 (Luiten et al., 2020; Bernardin & Stiefelhagen, 2008; Ristani et al., 2016); commentary uses overlap metrics plus grounding acceptance; speech uses MOS, word/character error rate, and real-time factor. No single number captures the system. The literature's fragmentation across these metrics is itself a finding: papers rarely report a combined evaluation, which makes cross-paper comparison difficult.

## 6. Speech synthesis and prosody

Neural TTS has progressed from autoregressive spectrogram prediction to fast, controllable synthesis. Tacotron 2 demonstrated near-professional naturalness but was not real-time and offered little style control (Shen et al., 2018). FastSpeech 2 introduced non-autoregressive synthesis with explicit duration, pitch, and energy prediction (Ren et al., 2021). VITS combined a conditional VAE, normalising flows, and adversarial training for parallel waveform generation (Kim et al., 2021). StyleTTS 2 added style diffusion, enabling style selection without reference audio (Li et al., 2023). A recent survey organises controllable TTS along emotion, prosody, timbre, and duration axes (Xie et al., 2024).

Sports commentary imposes requirements that general TTS benchmarks do not capture: excitement must rise with the event, timing must fit the action, and the voice must remain intelligible over crowd noise. Fish Audio's S1 family offers emotion and tone markers and reports low real-time factors on a high-end GPU (Fish Audio, 2025), but it is a vendor report and has not been evaluated on sports commentary. To our knowledge, there is no peer-reviewed benchmark for sports-commentary TTS or for event-conditioned prosody. The project therefore treats TTS as a production component and evaluates it with MOS/RTF-style measures, while acknowledging that this is an open evaluation gap rather than a solved problem.

## 7. Synthesis and positioning

The literature supports four conclusions. First, perception is mature enough to supply useful facts: action spotting and tracking have strong benchmarks, and jersey-based team identification plus possession estimation is feasible without labelled identities. Second, commentary generation is moving from templates to neural and LLM systems, but alignment quality and grounding remain the limiting factors. Third, factual validation is under-developed in football commentary: overlap metrics dominate, and no dedicated faithfulness benchmark exists. Fourth, end-to-end systems are rarely reproducible on consumer hardware, and TTS for sports prosody is largely unstudied.

The present project is positioned at the intersection of these gaps. It separates a deterministic fact layer from surface realisation, validates generated lines against that layer, falls back to grounded templates, and logs every training run in ClearML. Its perception stack is deliberately lightweight — a fine-tuned YOLO detector, ByteTrack, jersey clustering, and a score-bug reader — rather than a full spotting network. Its language model is a small GPT-2 with LoRA rather than a 7B MLLM. These choices trade peak quality for auditability and deployability. The literature predicts the trade-off: a lightweight system will be less accurate on rare events and less fluent than a large model, but it can be inspected, reproduced, and run end-to-end on a single consumer GPU. The project's contribution is therefore not a new state-of-the-art score on SoccerNet; it is an auditable, reproducible instantiation of the full broadcast-to-speech pipeline, with explicit failure modes and a grounding layer that the commentary literature rarely exposes.
